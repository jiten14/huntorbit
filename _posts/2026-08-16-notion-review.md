Notion has spent the last few years trying to be everything at once — a docs tool, a database, a project tracker, and lately, an AI assistant bolted on top of all of it. That ambition is exactly what makes it worth reviewing again in 2026: has it held onto what made it great, or has it buckled under its own feature list?

We spent three weeks running an actual small team's daily workflow through Notion — project tracking, a content calendar, a shared knowledge base, and a handful of personal to-do lists — to find out.

## Where it still wins

The core editing experience remains the best in its class. Blocks are flexible enough to build almost any layout without fighting the tool, databases are genuinely powerful once you understand relations and rollups, and the template ecosystem means most teams never have to start from a blank page.

Notion's database views — table, board, calendar, gallery, timeline — cover most project-management needs without a separate tool. Switching between them on the same underlying data, rather than maintaining duplicate boards, is still Notion's single best structural idea.

> "It's the rare tool that scales from a personal to-do list to a company wiki without ever feeling like the wrong tool for either job."

## Where it slows you down

Performance on large databases is the most common complaint we ran into, and it held up in our testing — a database past a few thousand rows with several relations noticeably lags, especially on the desktop app. The AI features, while genuinely useful for drafting and summarizing, add a subscription tier that changes the pricing math for teams that don't need it.

- The free plan comfortably covers most individual use cases
- Team plans get expensive quickly once you're paying for AI add-ons across every seat
- Offline mode has improved but still lags behind local-first competitors like Obsidian

## Notion vs. the alternatives

If your team's main need is lightweight project tracking, a purpose-built tool like Linear or Trello will feel faster day-to-day. If it's pure documentation, a simpler wiki tool might be less overhead. Notion's advantage shows up specifically when a team wants *one* system instead of three — the cost of that flexibility is a steeper initial setup and, at scale, real performance trade-offs.

## Pricing, in plain terms

Notion's free tier is generous enough for individuals and very small teams to run indefinitely. Paid plans scale per-seat, with AI features priced as an additional add-on rather than bundled in. Because pricing structures shift fairly often, it's worth checking Notion's own pricing page for current numbers before budgeting — what we can say confidently is that the jump from "free" to "team, with AI" is the point where most people start comparison shopping.

If you're choosing between Notion and a more specialized tool, the deciding factor usually comes down to whether your team wants one flexible system or several purpose-built ones. For most small-to-mid-size teams in 2026, Notion still earns its place.
